Critic

Learning from surprise

New: cells that predict how good or bad the next moments will be, and a dopamine cell whose firing is the surprise: what happened minus what was predicted, a burst above its resting rate for better, a pause for worse.

Result: in a world whose poisons change, creatures with the critic live better than creatures taught by pain alone, and after the change they still tell the kinds apart by their look. In the lab the signal moves from the pain to its warning, as in recordings of dopamine cells, and an aversion fades when the pain stops coming, which pain alone cannot do.

So far pain has been the teacher. Every painful bite strengthens the aversion to what the creature was looking at, and nothing weakens it again. That works as long as the world keeps its promises. In this stage it does not: from tick 800 of a life on, the poison bushes are good and the good ones poison. A teacher that only speaks when it hurts cannot say “this is no longer bad”, so a creature taught by pain keeps avoiding what is now its best food, and goes on eating what now hurts it until each new pain has been learned.

The new teacher is a critic. It keeps an expectation and teaches with the difference. An expected pain teaches nothing new; a pain that fails to come is a pleasant surprise, and that undoes the aversion.

The circuit

  • The dopamine cell fires at a steady resting rate. Its rate minus that rest is the surprise: above it, things went better than expected; below it, worse. Taste excites it. Pain excites the habenula, which silences it.
  • Value cells in the ventral striatum read the pallium and learn, within the life, how good (value_app) and how bad (value_av) the next moments will be when a bush looks like this. Like all striatal cells they inhibit. The good ones learn from bursts, the bad ones from pauses: the two learning rules of the striatum’s two cell types.
  • Each prediction reaches the dopamine cell twice. Fast, through steadily active cells of the pallidum that it inhibits (ventral_pallidum, gp_habenular): a rising good prediction releases dopamine at once, a rising bad one releases the habenula. And slowly, directly, a moment late. Fast minus slow is the change of prediction, so the surprise is taste − pain + prediction now − prediction a moment ago: when things go as predicted, the terms cancel.
  • The surprise teaches the value cells, so the predictions improve, and every synapse that pain taught before (look and pallium onto the bad cells): a bad surprise strengthens the aversion, a good one weakens it.

Biology

Dopamine cells in the midbrain fire a burst when something turns out better than expected and pause when it turns out worse. In the classic recordings in monkeys they fire at a drop of juice; once a light reliably announces the juice, they fire at the light instead; and when the juice is then left out, they pause at the moment it should have come. This is the teaching signal of temporal-difference learning, a method from machine learning that predicted these recordings.

How the expectation is subtracted is the work of the basal ganglia. The ventral striatum (nucleus accumbens) learns what a sight predicts and reaches the dopamine cells in two ways: directly, with slow inhibition, and through the ventral pallidum, whose steady inhibition of the dopamine cells it lifts. Bad news takes a parallel route through a part of the pallidum that drives the habenula, which pauses the dopamine cells; it was described in the lamprey, the oldest living vertebrate line. The same striatum, pallidum and dopamine signal will later choose actions.

Insects run the same scheme in the mushroom body (the counterpart of the pallium), with separate dopamine cells for reward and for punishment. Mammals mostly signal both with one population of cells, as this model does.

The world

The world of the pallium stage made harder, so that learning cannot be done without. A bush carries a single berry, and a poison berry costs as much food as a good one gives: biting every bush once to see gains nothing, only judging a bush by its look does. Meals are small and the body burns slowly, so one bad bite never kills and a creature has time for many lessons. For the first half of the evolution the kinds keep their meaning; from then on, at tick 800 of every life, the two poison kinds of bush turn good and the two good kinds turn poison, their looks unchanged.

Did it work?

The critic pays, alone and when the two kinds share a world. Without learning half the fitness is lost: in this world a creature that does not learn which bushes hurt cannot live. Of the critic’s cells, those that predict bad things (value_av) are used; those that predict good things (value_app) are not. The critic’s lesson here is “this no longer hurts”: a pain that was predicted and does not come.

After the switch only the critic’s creatures take up the new food. Early in life both kinds of creature bite the bushes that hurt less often than the good ones. After tick 800 both soon stop eating the bushes that turned poison, since every bite of them hurts. But the bushes that turned good are another matter: the critic’s creatures eat more and more of them, while the pain teacher’s go on avoiding them, held back by an aversion nothing in them can undo.

In the lab

The theory makes sharp predictions, and they can be tested on one creature at a time. A new bush is put in front of it; it bites three times and feels pain each time; after a pause the next trial begins.

The surprise moves to the warning. At the first painful meal the signal drops at the bites. After a few meals it drops as soon as the bush appears, and less at the bites. When the pain is then left out, the signal turns positive where the pain should have been, as in the monkey recordings.

Extinction: when the pain stops, the critic’s aversion fades to a fraction over twelve meals; the pain teacher’s does not change at all, since nothing in it can weaken a lesson.

NoteWhat it took
  • A world where the look matters. In the first version of this stage (the pallium’s world, six berries to a bush, reversal from the start) neither kind of creature judged a bush by its look before biting: they bit every bush once and stopped if it hurt. The critic paid there only by a learned appetite for bushes in general. A learned aversion was there, but too weak to stop a bite: one painful bite is one tick of pain, and the lesson stayed below what the aversive cells need to fire. Evolution then turned the aversion learning down.
  • A harsh world, with room for mistakes. A poison bite costing as much as a good one gives makes trying everything worthless. Making it dearer alone killed the creatures before they could learn; smaller meals and a slower metabolism spread the same risk over many decisions.
  • Learning to start with. The aversion learning starts ten times stronger than the pallium stage left it, so that the first generation has learners to select; evolution kept it high.
  • A calm start. With the swap from the first generation, the lesson that the look matters had to be learned in a world that keeps changing its mind; with fifty generations without it, caution by look evolves first.
  • Both paths. With only the slow, direct inhibition the dopamine cell compares what happens with what was predicted for this very moment. A known poison bush in view then predicts pain before any pain comes, and that reads as relief: on every step of the approach it unlearned the aversion, and the creatures did worse than with no predictions at all. The fast path through the pallidum turns the comparison into one between now and a moment ago, so a view that stays the same is no surprise.
  • A first version of this page computed the surprise with a formula instead of cells. The creatures did about as well with it as with these; the cells are kept because they are how the brain does it and because the basal ganglia that follow are built from the same parts.
  • Counting the cells. Each value cell has its own synapse onto the next cell, so a prediction’s strength has to be shared out over them; at first it was not, the predictions counted three times over, and evolution damped them by pushing the horizon to its end.
  • The right moment. The surprise of a tick is about the step that led to it, so it changes the synapses that were active a tick earlier (real synapses are changed by dopamine that arrives a second or two late).
  • Steady cells keep their strength. Evolution may tune how strongly the predictions reach the dopamine cell, but not the outputs of the steadily active cells: changing those moved the dopamine cell’s resting rate, and a surprise that never settles at zero.
  • A short horizon. Nothing tells the creature how long a pain will last while the bush is in view, so the predictions look only a few ticks ahead. A sense of time since a warning is for a later stage.
  • Wiring of its own. The pallium’s random wiring belongs to the creature’s place in the population, not to its genes, so no lineage can inherit a lucky code.
