Background

Brains and evolution in brief

This book assumes no biology and little machine learning. This page explains the few ideas that every later page uses: what a neuron does, why some neurons excite and others inhibit, how to read the brain diagrams, the three ways a connection gets its strength, and how evolution and the experiments are run. The parts of real brains that the stages model are introduced on their own pages. A glossary at the end collects the terms.

If you already know neural networks, the callout below says what is different here.

TipIf you know artificial neural networks
  • The units are rate neurons: each has one activity between 0 and 1, updated once per tick, and the network is recurrent (a cell can feed back onto itself through others).
  • Every cell is either excitatory or inhibitory: all its outgoing weights are positive, or all are negative.
  • There is no backpropagation and no gradient. Weights come from three places: some are designed, most are found by a genetic algorithm over generations, and a few change within a life by a local learning rule.
  • The networks are small (tens to a few hundred cells), and most cells have a fixed, named meaning, so that you can point at one and say what it is for.
  • The question is not “what is the best network for this task” but “which circuit does evolution put to use, given the brain it already has”.

Neurons and synapses

A neuron is a cell that collects signals from other neurons and passes on a signal of its own. In this book a neuron is described by one number, its activity: 0 when it is silent, 1 when it fires as fast as it can.

Neurons are connected by synapses. A synapse has a strength (in machine learning: a weight). Every tick, a neuron adds up the activity of the neurons that reach it, each multiplied by the strength of its synapse, and its new activity follows that sum, squashed into the range from 0 to 1 (a negative sum gives 0). That is nearly all of it.

The words neuron and cell mean the same thing in this book.

Exciting and inhibiting

A synapse can push its target up or down. An excitatory neuron raises the activity of every neuron it reaches; an inhibitory neuron lowers it. A real neuron is one or the other, never both (this is called Dale’s law), and the brains in this book follow that rule.

Inhibition is not just “the minus sign”. It is how a brain says not now, and most of the circuits in this book are built from three small patterns:

  • Excitation passes a signal on: this is food, so bite.
  • A gate is an inhibitory cell that holds an action back until a condition is met. In the valence stage, a cell that fires whenever nothing is within reach holds the bite shut, so that wanting to eat does not turn into biting the air.
  • Disinhibition switches a gate off, and that releases the action. Brains use it a lot: rather than pushing an action into being, they keep many actions held back and lift the hold on one.

A fourth pattern appears later: competition, where cells inhibit each other so that only the strongest few stay active.

Reading a brain diagram

Every stage page shows the brain of that stage. Here is the brain of the valence stage, the second one:

  • A box is a group of neurons with one job, called a region: the eyes, the motor neurons that move the creature, a cell for pain. Its name says what it is for.
  • An arrow between two boxes is a projection: all the synapses from one region onto another. The end of the arrow says what it does: an arrowhead excites, a bar inhibits, and a dot (on arrows from the senses) means a mix of both.
  • The colour of an arrow says where its strengths come from (next section), and its width how strong it is overall.
  • Under a box’s name: whether its cells excite or inhibit, and how many there are. The coloured backgrounds group regions that belong together.
  • A box outlined in green is new in this stage. The diagram opens on the new circuit alone; the button everything shows the whole brain. The ganglion, an evolved tangle with no named cells, is drawn as one box.

Point at a box or an arrow, or tap it on a phone, for details. You do not need to follow every arrow. Each page says in words which arrows matter, and the rest is the brain of earlier stages.

Where strengths come from

arrow origin changes example
dark designed (hard-wired): we wire it, evolution may only make it stronger or weaker as a whole over generations, as a whole pain → the “this is bad” cells
grey evolved: which synapses exist and how strong each is is inherited, with mutations over generations, synapse by synapse eyes → motor neurons
purple learned: it starts where evolution left it and changes during a life within one life the look of a berry → “this is bad”, after it hurt

Designed connections give a cell a fixed meaning that later stages can rely on: a cell that is wired to pain and nothing else means pain. Evolved connections are where evolution finds its own solutions, often in ways that are hard to read. Learned connections are the only memory a creature has of its own life.

Learning within a life

The learning in this book follows one recipe, found in real animals from sea slugs to mammals. A synapse that was just active leaves a short trace. When something important happens, such as pain, a teaching signal is sent to many synapses at once, and only the ones that carry a trace change. Pain says something bad happened; the trace says and this is what was there.

Modulators

Some cells do not talk to one partner through a synapse. They release a neuromodulator, a substance that spreads and reaches many cells at once, and that is cleared slowly. Serotonin and dopamine are the best-known. Modulators set the mood of a brain more than its next move: because they linger, they let an event act on behaviour for a while after it is over. In the diagrams they are drawn in pink.

Evolution

Evolution here is a genetic algorithm. A genome is what a creature inherits: the strengths of its evolved synapses and a few settings, such as how fast it learns. A population of genomes is born into a world, each as a few identical creatures. The fitness of a genome is how well its creatures did: here, roughly, how long they lived with a full stomach. The genomes that did best have children, each from two parents, with small random changes (mutations). That is one generation, and a stage runs for about a hundred of them.

Nobody tells evolution what a circuit is for. If a new cell helps, the genomes that use it have more children; if it does not, evolution may turn it down or ignore it.

Reading the experiments

Each stage asks one question: does the new circuit get used? Three tools answer it.

  • Main against control. Main is the new brain. The control is the brain of the previous stage, in the same new world, starting from the same population. Whatever separates them is the new circuit, so every comparison on a stage page is new brain against old brain.
  • Lesions. At the end, a region of the evolved brain is silenced and the creatures live again. If they do much worse, they were using it. This is the main evidence on every page.
  • Seeds. Evolution is noisy: one run can end almost anywhere. So every stage is run three times from different random starts (seeds). A result counts only when it holds in all three.

Results are given as a mean over the seeds with a standard error (±): roughly, how far the mean might move if the experiment were run again. A difference smaller than about twice its standard error could be luck. How a stage is judged on the introduction page says more.

Glossary

term meaning
creature one simulated individual; animal always means a real one
tick one step of the simulation, about a second of behaviour
look what a creature sees of a thing: a short list of numbers, like a colour
neuron, cell a unit with an activity between 0 (silent) and 1 (firing fast)
synapse, strength a connection between two neurons, and how much it counts
excitatory, inhibitory raises or lowers the activity of the neurons it reaches
region a group of neurons with one job, a box in the diagram
projection all synapses from one region onto another, an arrow in the diagram
hard-wired designed; evolution tunes only its overall strength
evolved inherited synapse by synapse, shaped by selection
learned, plastic changes within a life
motor neurons the cells that move the creature: step, turn, bite
motor programme a cell that triggers a whole action, such as biting
gate an inhibitory cell that holds an action back until a condition is met
disinhibition releasing an action by silencing its gate
valence sorting things into good (appetitive) and bad (aversive)
unconditioned stimulus something that matters without learning, such as pain or taste
adaptation a sense reporting change rather than level: what stays the same fades
modulator a substance released by a few cells that acts on many, slowly
genome what a creature inherits
fitness what selection rewards: here, the well-fed lifetime
generation one round of living, selecting and breeding
seed one independent run of a stage, from its own random start
main, control the new brain, and the previous brain in the same new world
lesion silencing a region to see whether the creatures need it
