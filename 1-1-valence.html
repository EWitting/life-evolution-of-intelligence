1.1 Valence: good and bad

The animal of the previous stage steers by reflex. What it sees is wired, through a small ganglion, straight to its motor neurons. It has no way of knowing what happened after it bit something.

This stage gives it that. It adds a handful of neurons that stand for this is good and this is bad, and puts food in the world that is bad to eat.

The world: two kinds of berry are poison

There are now six kinds of berry bush instead of four. They look alike, in the way that different berries look alike: each kind has the shape of a bush and its own colour.

Four kinds are food. Two are poison, and always the same two. Eating a poison berry empties the stomach by about as much as a good berry fills it, and it hurts for a moment.

Because the same two kinds are poison in every life, nothing has to be learned during a life. Which colours to avoid can be inherited. Learning it within a life is the subject of a later stage.

The biology: valence

In small animals, most sensory neurons fall into two classes. Some respond to things worth approaching, such as the smell of food. Others respond to things worth avoiding, such as a harmful chemical or a hard touch on the nose. The two classes converge onto a few interneurons that bias the animal towards carrying on or turning away.

Valence is the name for this sorting of the world into good and bad. In the worm C. elegans, a much-studied animal with 302 neurons, harmful stimuli do two things at once: they trigger a turn, and they stop the pumping of the throat with which the worm feeds. Max Bennett argues that this sorting is what the first brains were for.

The worm is only a stand-in. The animal that this chapter is about, the common ancestor of animals with a left and a right side, left no nervous system to look at.

The circuit

Five new groups of neurons, each with a meaning that is fixed by its wiring:

  • valence_app, the appetitive cells: active when something is good. The sense of taste is hard-wired to them, and reaches the brain nowhere else.
  • valence_av, the aversive cells: active when something is bad. Pain is hard-wired to them, and reaches the brain nowhere else.
  • grasp, a motor programme: when it fires, the animal bites what is in front of it.
  • no_touch, a gate: an inhibitory cell that is active whenever nothing is directly ahead, and holds grasp shut.
  • no_feed: an inhibitory pair driven by the aversive cells, which blocks the bite.

Taste and pain are unconditioned signals: they mean good and bad without any experience. But they arrive only after the bite. To act in time, the value cells also get input from the eyes, and that input is evolved. Over generations, the look of a poison berry comes to excite the aversive cells before anything has been eaten.

Value does not move the animal by itself. The appetitive cells excite grasp, but grasp can only fire when no_touch is silent, that is, when something is within reach. Releasing an action by switching off its inhibition is called disinhibition, and it is a common arrangement in real nervous systems. Here it makes a value turn into the right action for the situation: bite when the good thing is in front of the mouth, and not before.

The aversive cells do what the worm does. They drive the motor neurons for turning, and through no_feed they shut down biting.

Watching it work

Below is one animal from the last generation, around the moment it bites a poison berry.

Step through it one tick at a time. At the bite, nothing in the brain has changed yet: the pain arrives with the next tick. Then the pain sense is on, both aversive cells fire, no_feed switches on and grasp falls silent. The animal turns away. A few ticks later the pain has faded and it is eating again.

Nothing was learned here. The animal will react in the same way the next time. What stops it from biting poison often is the evolved input from the eyes, which acts before the bite.

Results

Is main worse than control?

The control is the brain of stage 1.0 in this world. It cannot feel pain or taste at all, so it can only avoid poison if evolution happens to wire the look of the poison berries to turning away.

In principle the control can end up where main does. The poison looks the same in every generation, so pain tells the animal nothing that the colour of the bush does not, and a brain without pain can evolve “this colour, turn away” in its reflex wiring. So the gap between the curves does not say what pain is worth. It says how much easier the value cells make the solution to find: main has a ready path from the look of a berry to a cell that turns the animal away and stops the bite, and it starts out with a reaction to the pain itself.

Whether a control finds the solution within the generations it is given is a matter of luck. Under an earlier food economy two of three controls did, late and suddenly, after dozens of generations without progress. If a grey curve above stays flat, that control had not found it yet.

The three rows measure the same thing with less and less noise. The first is taken from the generations as they ran. The second gives every final population the same eight worlds. In the third, main and control animals live together and compete for the same bushes, so a difference cannot come from one population having had an easier world.

Is the circuit used?

Read this chart region by region. A short bar means the animals depended on that region. A bar near 100 means they did as well without it, and three dots far apart mean the seeds disagree.

The two halves of the circuit are not used equally. The aversive side, valence_av and no_feed, is what the animals depend on. The appetitive side, valence_app and grasp, matters much less: the reflexes of the previous stage already bring the animal to food and make it bite.

NoteWhat did not work: appetite on a full stomach

In the first runs of this stage the animals were born with a full stomach, and the appetitive side was not used at all: silencing valence_app, no_touch or grasp left them as well off as before. A full stomach lasted for most of a life, so there was little to gain from eating well. Born a quarter full, the animals depend on the appetitive side a little, as the chart shows.

One fault remains. The appetitive side makes the animal bite whenever food is in reach, hungry or not, and a large share of all bites lands on a stomach that is nearly full. Nothing in this brain can sense the stomach. That comes with a later stage, which adds a cell for hunger.
