Association: learning what is bad

Until now everything the animal knows was put there by evolution. That works for poison that looks the same in every generation. It cannot work for food that is new.

This stage adds the simplest kind of learning from experience: after a bite that hurts, the look of what was bitten comes to mean bad.

The world: nothing is familiar

There are four kinds of berry bush, and all four are new in every life. Each life draws a new colour for every kind, and draws which two of the four are poison. Nothing an ancestor learned about colours is worth inheriting.

The bushes still look like berry bushes. A new kind differs from the others in its colour, not in its shape, the way a new berry is recognisable as a berry.

A poison berry costs about what a good berry gives, and it hurts for one tick. Half of all bushes are poison, so an animal that cannot learn pays that price on every second bush it tries, for as long as it lives. Leaving everything unknown alone is not an option either: there is nothing else to eat.

There are no duds in this world. What the eyes learned to do in the previous stage stays, and this stage builds on it.

NoteWhat did not work: a world where learning was hardly worth it

The first versions of this world kept a familiar, safe kind of berry as half of all bushes, so that an animal that learned nothing would still get by. It got by very well: it ate about eleven good berries for every poison one. A perfect learner would have gained less than a tenth of its food. With so little to win, a lesson had to be nearly free of side effects to pay at all, and evolution switched learning off whenever it was not.

Removing the familiar food turned this around. In the world above, a perfect learner would save about a quarter of what it eats.

The biology: learning by association

A sea slug that is shocked after a touch learns to pull back from the touch. A rat that gets sick after a new food avoids that food afterwards. This is associative learning: something neutral (the touch, the taste) comes to predict something that already matters (the shock, the sickness), and the animal starts to respond to the first as it would to the second.

The synapse-level picture, worked out in the sea slug Aplysia and the fruit fly, has three parts. A sensory neuron that was just active leaves a short-lived trace at its synapses. A teaching signal, released when something bad happens, reaches many synapses at once. Only the synapses that carry a fresh trace are strengthened. Pain says something bad happened; the trace says and this is what was there.

The circuit

One new cell and one change to existing synapses:

  • us_pain, the teacher: driven by pain alone. When it fires, it broadcasts a teaching signal.
  • The synapses from the eyes’ look inputs onto the aversive cells valence_av, which evolution shaped in the previous stage, can now also change during a life. A synapse grows when its input was just active and the teacher fires.

The aversive cells already turn the animal away and block the bite. So nothing else has to change: once the look of a berry excites them, the animal treats that berry as it treats the poison it was born knowing.

What exactly gets blamed

A bite hurts, and something has to take the blame. Getting this right turned out to be most of the work of this stage, and three things were needed, the first of which the animals already bring with them.

The lesson is about what sets the food apart. All berry bushes look alike in most respects. If pain strengthened the synapses of the whole look, one bad red berry would make the animal wary of every berry. This is where the adapting eyes of the previous stage pay a second time: they pass on the difference between the look right now and what they usually see. What all bushes share cancels out before it reaches a synapse. What is left to take the blame is the colour.

The pain is brief. While pain faded over several ticks, the teacher was still firing after the animal had turned away, and by then its eyes were on the next bush. The animal learned to distrust the neighbour of the poison as much as the poison.

Every learned synapse exists. The look synapses were sparse, as evolution had left them, and a synapse that is not there cannot learn. In this stage all of them are present, the new ones starting at zero.

NoteWhat did not work: unlearning by tasting again

An earlier design had a second cell that lowered the aversion whenever something the animal distrusted turned out to taste fine. It worked when meals were fed to the brain in a test. In a living animal it never fired: aversion blocks the bite, so food that is distrusted is never tasted again, and the mistake cannot be found out.

This is why the lesson has to land on the right food in the first place.

Watching it work

One animal from the last generation, at two moments of its life.

First, its first bite of a kind of berry that is poison in this life. Before the bite the aversive cells are silent and grasp fires. One tick after the bite the pain arrives, the teacher us_pain fires, and the aversive cells and no_feed switch on by reflex, as in the previous stage. What cannot be seen is the change the teacher leaves behind in the synapses.

Later in the same life the animal comes up to a bush of the same kind. Nothing hurts now, and yet the aversive cells fire and no_feed holds the bite back. The animal moves on. It learned this; no ancestor could have told it.

Results

Main against control

The control is the brain of the previous stage in this world, adapting eyes included: it feels pain and turns away from it, but it cannot change. It pays for every poison bush it tries, every time.

Is the circuit used?

no_plasticity freezes every synapse at its inherited strength, and us_pain silences the teacher. If the animals do clearly worse without either, they depend on what they learn during their life.

How a life is spent

Few animals live out the long life this world offers: half of what they try is poison, and nothing tells an animal that it is getting hungry.
