Preparing the ground

Chapter 1 ended with an animal that steers, avoids what hurts, learns which food to distrust, looks after two needs and carries a mood for a while. This chapter builds the brain of an early fish on top of it, and most of what it adds is about learning.

Before anything is added, the ground is prepared. This stage puts no new part into the brain. It tunes what chapter 1 built, so that the stages after it have something to stand on: a world in which every part of the animal has work to do, moods that fit that world, and eyes that see a little more of it. The question is only whether the animals settle in, and whether they still use everything they have.

A world with work for every part

The last two things chapter 1 added pulled in opposite directions.

Learning which food is poison pays where good and poison bushes stand side by side. The two lasting states, to keep going after pain and to stay after a meal, pay where every kind of bush grows in its own patch, because then what just happened says something about what is near. In a patch of one kind there is nothing left to learn: the first bite says everything about the neighbours. In open country where the kinds are evenly mixed there is nothing to stay for or to flee from.

The stages of this chapter are about learning, but they build on the lasting states too, so the world has to pay for both. Several worlds were tried with the animals of chapter 1.

With the lasting states as chapter 1 built them, no world did it. In open country, and in patches with every kind mixed inside, the animals learned and let the lasting states go. In patches that were nearly pure they stayed and fled and stopped learning. In between, each line of descent settled on one of the two and dropped the other, and which one differed from seed to seed. Knowing poison by its look and staying where the food was good are two ways of doing the same thing, and evolution kept one.

What changed that is described in the next section. The world that came out of it has patches that are mostly one kind, with about a third of the bushes in a patch being of the other kinds. It is also larger, for the same number of animals; the reason is under Results.

Two steps

The animals were not moved into that world at once. They first spent a while in open country, and only then in the patches.

The order matters. In the pure patches at the end of chapter 1, learning had nothing to do, and a part that is not used drifts: the animals arrived with learning set too strong, so that a lesson about one bush spread to bushes that only resembled it. When such animals were put straight into the patches, it was easier for evolution to switch learning off than to repair it, and in some lines of descent that is what happened. In open country there is no other way to tell a poison bush than by its look, so there learning was repaired. After that, the patches kept it.

The figures on this page are from the second step.

The food is as before: four kinds of berry bush, new in every life, half of them poison.

Moods that build up

In chapter 1 each lasting state was released in full by a single event. One poison bite was a full alarm, and one good bite was the full urge to stay. That fits a patch of one kind, where one bite is all the evidence there is.

In a patch of mixed kinds one bite says little. A good bush can stand in poor ground, and a poison bush in good ground. Several bites close together say much more: this is mostly good ground, or mostly bad.

So the two states now build up. Every bite adds a little, and what was added fades with time. The animal flees only when more than one poison bite has come in a short while, and settles only after more good bites than one bush can give. A single bite of either kind passes without a change of mood.

This is closer to how such signals behave in a body. A modulator is released in small amounts and cleared slowly, so its level at any moment is a running sum of what happened lately, not a record of the last event alone.

No cell is added for this. The same two modulators and the same cells are used; only how a modulator’s level follows its release is different, and the cells that read it have a threshold. The thresholds were set by hand and the fading times were left to evolution.

A little more to see

The animal now looks in nine directions where it had five, and sees one step further. This is not a new part of the brain. No cells are added, and nothing new is done with what the eyes see. Every part that listened to the five old directions listens to all nine, with a connection of its own from each.

The four new directions sit between the old ones. They do not start silent. Each new connection starts as the average of the same connection from its two neighbours, so that the animal makes sense of what it sees there from its first tick, and all connections from the eyes are made weaker by the same factor, because nine send nearly twice as much as five. When the new directions started silent instead, the animals did worse after the same number of generations.

The run

This stage has no control, because there is no new circuit to test. The comparison is with the population the second step started from: the animals as they came out of open country, with moods that build up, on their first day in the patches.

The curve of a run is hard to read here, because every generation lives in a new world and the worlds differ a great deal. The fair comparison puts the starting population and the evolved one in the same worlds. That is the table below, in the row for this stage’s world.

Room

The same table shows why the world was made larger. With the same animals and the same density of bushes, both populations do better when there is more room, and the better population gains the most.

The reason is in what is left standing. A good bush is emptied in a few bites. A poison bush loses one berry and keeps the rest. Where many animals forage close together, the bushes that still carry berries are more and more the poison ones, and the good ones run out nearby although the world as a whole grows plenty. A population that forages better does this to itself faster. In a small world that hides part of an improvement, so the world of this chapter has four times the area of the worlds of chapter 1. Larger still would help less and less, and more and more animals would simply live out their whole life.

Is everything used?

Yes. In every seed the animals do worse without learning, worse without the urge to stay after a meal, and worse without the alarm.

None of the losses is large, and that is what one would expect where parts share a job. In these patches both the look of a bush and the ground it stands on say something about it, and an animal that loses one of the two still has the other. Learning counts for less here than it did in open country. That is the price of the patches, and it is accepted: the stages that follow make learning better, and they build on the lasting states as well.

fed and settle are two links of one chain, so they fall together. rest is the mode through which both the urge to stay and the search for warmth act, so silencing it costs more than either alone.

How a life is spent

These animals are on the move for a small part of their life. Much of the rest they stand where they have eaten and grasp at bushes that are empty for now. Staying pays, because the good bushes around them grow back, but most of that grasping gains nothing.

What is still missing sets the task for the stages that follow. The animal learns what to avoid and not what is worth wanting. A lesson about one thing spreads to things that look like it. And nothing in its brain weighs whether a bite is worth taking before it takes it.
